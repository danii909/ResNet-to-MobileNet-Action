## 1. Introduction and Problem Definition

Video action recognition is computationally expensive due to the extra temporal dimension. State-of-the-art models (e.g., 3D CNNs, Video Transformers) require computational resources that are incompatible with mobile and edge devices. 

The objective of this project is to compress a heavy, highly accurate **Teacher** model (**3D ResNet-50**) into an ultra-lightweight **Student** model (**MobileNet3D**) on the **UCF-101** dataset (101 classes, video clips of 24 frames, resolution 112×112).

### Preventing Data Leakage: Group-Aware Split
To ensure a fair and realistic evaluation of performance and to avoid data leakage, the dataset was partitioned using a **group-aware split** with a fixed seed of `42`. This technique guarantees that videos belonging to the same group (e.g., recorded by the same user or in the same environment) are not shared across the training, validation, and test sets.

## 2. Architectures and Computational Footprint

The two networks exhibit high structural heterogeneity and a significant capacity gap:

*   **Teacher (3D ResNet-50 / `slow_r50`):** Based on the SlowFast architecture (slow pathway). Pre-trained on Kinetics-400 and fine-tuned on UCF-101. Uses standard high-dimensional 3D convolutions.
    *   **Memory Footprint:** $\sim$128 MB
    *   **Performance Limit (Upper Bound):** **88.82%** Test Top-1 (92.36% Val Top-1).
*   **Student (MobileNet3D):** An adaptation of the MobileNetV2 architecture to 3D convolutions. It leverages *3D depthwise separable convolutions* and inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks to minimize FLOPs.
    *   **Memory Footprint:** $\sim$9.5 MB (compression factor of **$\sim$13.5×**).
    *   **Performance from Scratch (Baseline):** **59.48%** Test Top-1 (59.18% Val Top-1).

The initial gap between the Teacher and the Student trained from scratch is **29.34 percentage points**, justifying the use of Knowledge Distillation to recover accuracy without increasing the model's footprint on the target device.

## 3. Logit-Based Knowledge Distillation (Vanilla KD)

### 3.1 Mathematical Formulation
Classical distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) forces the student to mimic the probability distribution produced by the teacher using a combined loss function:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \alpha \cdot T^2 \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\left(\sigma\left(\frac{z^S}{T}\right) \;\parallel\; \sigma\left(\frac{z^T}{T}\right)\right) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(z^S, y)$$

Where:
*   $z^S, z^T$ are the logits generated by the student and teacher, respectively.
*   $\sigma$ represents the softmax function.
*   $T$ is the **temperature**, a hyperparameter that controls the smoothness of the probability distributions.
*   $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
*   $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$ is the standard cross-entropy loss computed on the hard ground truth labels $y$.
*   $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is the balancing coefficient (set to `0.7` by default).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Gradient Balancing:** Since logits scaled by the temperature $1/T$ reduce the magnitude of the gradients produced by the $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$ loss by a factor of $1/T^2$, it is mathematically necessary to multiply the $D_{\text{KL}}$ term by **$T^2$** to keep the gradients balanced relative to the gradients of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$.

### 3.2 Temperature Ablation Study
While keeping $\alpha = 0.7$ fixed, the temperature $T$ was varied to evaluate the effect of logit smoothing:

| Temperature ($T$) | Best Val Top-1 | Best Epoch | Train Acc | Test Top-1 | Test Top-5 | $\Delta$ vs Baseline |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| *Baseline Scratch* | *59.18%* | *---* | *---* | *59.48%* | *82.77%* | *---* |
| **$T = 1$** | 62.75% | 60 | 98.44% | 62.91% | 85.59% | +3.43% |
| **$T = 5$** | 63.12% | 57 | 95.87% | 62.07% | 87.02% | +2.59% |
| **$T = 8$** | 64.57% | 56 | 97.59% | 64.31% | 87.68% | +4.83% |
| **$T = 10$** | 64.85% | 60 | 98.28% | 64.47% | 87.44% | +4.99% |
| **$T = 20$ (Best)** | 65.18% | 59 | 99.05% | **65.85%** | **87.81%** | **+6.37%** |

### 3.3 Analysis of the Temperature Effect
*   With **low temperatures ($T=1$)**, the student receives highly concentrated distributions (similar to one-hot encodings). The student learns to imitate the teacher's primary choice but misses the latent inter-class relationships.
*   With **high temperatures ($T=20$)**, the softmax is "softened," revealing the relative similarity between negative classes (the so-called **dark knowledge**). For instance, the residual probability that a "Running" video resembles "High Jump" rather than "Writing" helps the student map complex decision boundaries. Consequently, the student reaches its peak performance of **65.85%** (+6.37 pp).

## 4. Feature-Based Distillation: Attention Transfer (AT)

While logit-based KD acts on the final output, Attention Transfer (Zagoruyko & Komodakis, 2017) aligns the activation maps of intermediate layers, teaching the student *where to look* within the video volume.

### 4.1 Identified and Corrected Bugs in Naive AT

#### A. Silent Block Mismatch (Key 5)
The original implementation placed forward hooks on blocks `[3, 4, 5]` of the Teacher. However, block 5 corresponds to the classification head (`ResNetBasicHead`), which returns a flat 2D tensor of shape `[B, 101]`, not a 5D feature map.
*   **Consequence:** Computing attention on a 2D vector caused the L2-normalization operation to return a vector of all `1.0`s. The MSE between two constants is zero, which silently zeroed out the gradient for the final distillation block.
*   **Fix:** Modified the teacher's intermediate block tracking to hook blocks `[2, 3, 4]`, pairing them correctly with stages `[2, 4, 6]` of MobileNet3D (which preserve space-time representations).

#### B. 1D Linear Interpolation Distortion
When spatial resolutions differed (e.g., Teacher `14×14` vs. Student `4×4`), the original code flattened the 3D map `[T, H, W]` to 1D before applying 1D linear interpolation.
*   **Consequence (Spatial-Temporal Collapse):** Applying 1D interpolation to a flattened vector mixes unrelated spatial and temporal coordinates. In 1D, the bottom-right pixel of frame $t$ is contiguous with the top-left pixel of frame $t+1$; interpolating between them introduces destructive noise.

### 4.2 Solution: Separate Spatial and Temporal Decomposition
To resolve the collapse, the 5D feature maps $F \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times T \times H \times W}$ are decomposed by computing two separate attention maps:

1.  **Spatial Attention ($A_{\text{spatial}}$):** Average over channels and the temporal dimension.
    $$A_{\text{spatial}}(F) = \text{L2\_normalize}\left(\text{mean}_{c,t}\left(F^2\right)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \cdot W}$$
    *Alignment:* Solved by reshaping the student map to $[B, 1, H_S, W_S]$, applying **2D bilinear interpolation** to match the teacher's $[B, 1, H_T, W_T]$ grid (preserving spatial grid structure), and flattening.
2.  **Temporal Attention ($A_{\text{temporal}}$):** Average over channels and spatial dimensions.
    $$A_{\text{temporal}}(F) = \text{L2\_normalize}\left(\text{mean}_{c,h,w}\left(F^2\right)\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times T}$$
    *Alignment:* Since the temporal stride is 1 for both models ($T_T = T_S = 24$), no interpolation is needed (direct noise-free transfer).

The total Attention Transfer loss is formulated as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{AT}} = \beta_s \mathcal{L}_{\text{spatial}} + \beta_t \mathcal{L}_{\text{temporal}}$$

Or, detailed across all matching blocks:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{AT}} = \beta_s \sum_i \text{MSE}\left(A^T_{\text{spatial},i}, A^S_{\text{spatial},i}\right) + \beta_t \sum_i \text{MSE}\left(A^T_{\text{temporal},i}, A^S_{\text{temporal},i}\right)$$

### 4.3 Analysis of the Results of AT
Despite the mathematical fixes, introducing AT underperformed compared to pure logit-based distillation (65.85%):
*   **Naive AT (Before):** **59.14%** (below baseline).
*   **Symmetric AT ($\beta_s=0.05, \beta_t=0.05$):** **64.58%**.
*   **Temporal-Only AT ($\beta_s=0, \beta_t=0.10$):** **65.03%**.

#### Physical Rationale for the Performance:
1.  **Attention Loss & Information Collapse (Channel Pooling Bottleneck):** Classical Attention Transfer aligns student and teacher maps of different dimensions by collapsing the channel axis (averaging over $C$). This projection functions as a destructive bottleneck: it merges and flattens the teacher's specialized filter responses (such as edge, texture, or direction detectors). The student receives basic spatial-temporal guidance on *where* or *when* to focus, but loses the dense semantic richness and the underlying justification of *why* those areas are critical.
2.  **Convolution Mismatch (Spatial Capacity Gap):** The teacher's standard 3D convolutions combine channels freely, generating rich attention maps. The student (MobileNet3D) uses *depthwise separable* convolutions, where channels are processed in isolation during spatial convolution. This structural limitation prevents the student from modeling the teacher's spatial attention; forcing it via AT loss acts as destructive noise.
3.  **Temporal Transferability:** The fact that the *Temporal-Only* variant recovers performance compared to the symmetric one confirms that the spatial signal acted as a harmful constraint, whereas the temporal evolution of the action is highly transferable.

## 5. Born-Again Networks (Iterative Self-Distillation)

The Born-Again Networks (BAN) framework (Furlanello et al., 2018) suggests that training a student of the same size as the teacher via distillation can lead to superior performance. This iterative self-distillation was evaluated over 3 generations:

| Generation | Teacher Source | Val Top-1 | Test Top-1 | $\Delta$ vs Gen 0 |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Gen 0 (KD $T=8$)** | ResNet-50 (Teacher) | 64.57% | 64.31% | --- |
| **Gen 1** | Gen 0 Student | 60.78% | 60.98% | -3.33% |
| **Gen 2** | Gen 1 Student | 61.20% | 60.43% | -3.88% |
| **Gen 3** | Gen 2 Student | 59.98% | 59.21% | -5.10% |

### Generational Collapse Analysis
The experiments revealed a progressive degradation (generational collapse), with Gen 3 dropping to **59.21%**, below the scratch baseline.
*   **Lack of an Oracle Teacher:** The BAN theory assumes a highly accurate teacher. When the initial teacher (Gen 0) has a modest accuracy (~64%), it lacks the discriminative capacity to guide subsequent training.
*   **Error Accumulation:** The models commit systematic errors (bias). In subsequent generations, the student not only copies correct knowledge but also **over-copies and amplifies the systematic errors of the previous teacher**, leading to overfitting of predictive artifacts.
*   **Uncalibrated Temperature:** Using $T=2.5$ for self-distillation reduces the entropy of the soft signal when the teacher has low confidence, reducing the richness of the dark knowledge transferred.

## 6. Cross-Frame Distillation: Asymmetric Temporal Sub-Sampling

For resource-constrained edge deployment, **Cross-Frame Knowledge Distillation** was implemented as a Pareto-optimal trade-off. In this configuration:
*   The **Teacher** processes the full **24-frame** clip to extract high-fidelity spatial-temporal representations.
*   The **Student** receives a sub-sampled **16-frame** version of the same clip. The sub-sampling is performed uniformly along the temporal dimension using `torch.linspace` (implemented on GPU in [temporal.py](file:///i:/Development%202.0/KD_Project/src/training/temporal.py)).
*   The Student is distilled using the optimal logit-based KD configuration ($T=20$, $\alpha=0.7$).

During evaluation and edge deployment, the Student only processes 16 frames, significantly reducing computational cost:

| Metric | 24f Student (KD $T=20$) | **16f Student (Cross-Frame)** | Difference |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Test Top-1 Accuracy** | 65.85% | **62.68%** | **-3.17%** |
| **CPU Latency** | Baseline (114 ms) | **54 ms** | **-52.6% (2.11× faster)** |
| **GPU Throughput** | Baseline (242 clips/s) | **408 clips/s** | **+68.8%** |

> **Key Takeaway:** By training with asymmetric frame lengths, the student learns to approximate the teacher's 24-frame representations from only 16 frames. This yields a **2.11× speedup** on CPU and a **+68.8% throughput boost** on GPU, at the cost of only **3.17 percentage points** in accuracy.

## 7. Numerical Optimization: Stability in FP16 (AMP)

During training in Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) mode, the Attention Transfer runs suffered from sudden numerical stability issues.

### The Problem: FP16 Overflow
During the attention map computation, squaring the feature maps ($F^2$) on tensors containing large activation values exceeded the maximum representable value in Float16 ($65,504$). This produced `NaN` or `Inf` values that propagated through the loss and destroyed the student's weights.

### The Solution
The attention map calculation is isolated, casting the tensors to **Float32 (single precision)** before the squaring operation, and then returning the normalized attention map (which falls within a safe range):
```python
# Explicit casting to float32 inside AMP to prevent FP16 overflow
f = features.float()                # Force conversion to float32
attn = (f ** 2).mean(dim=(1, 2))    # Safe computation in float32
```
This adjustment guaranteed numerical stability during training while preserving the performance benefits of AMP for the rest of the network.

## 8. Future Work

To overcome the capacity and structural bottlenecks discovered, two future directions are proposed:

### 8.1 Variable Temporal Stride for Fast Actions
To prevent *temporal over-smoothing* caused by high distillation temperatures ($T=20$) on fast, cyclic actions (e.g., *JumpingJack*, *TennisSwing*), the approach proposes incorporating a variable temporal stride:
*   **Dynamic Sampling:** Instead of training on clips extracted with a fixed stride ($s=1$), the temporal stride is randomly sampled at each epoch (e.g., $s \sim \mathcal{U}(\{1, 2, 3\})$).
*   **Temporal Invariance:** This forces the student to learn action representations at multiple speeds, making logit-based KD robust to soft-probability smoothing and preserving high-frequency motion transitions.

### 8.2 Feature Alignment via Learnable Adapter Blocks (AT)
The structural gap between the teacher's standard 3D convolutions and the student's depthwise separable 3D convolutions limits the effectiveness of spatial attention transfer. A promising solution consists in introducing adapter blocks:
*   **Learnable Adapters:** During training, lightweight $1\times1\times1$ convolutional modules $\Phi$ are inserted into the student's feature extraction pathway prior to attention map computation: $\hat{A}^S = \text{Attention}(\Phi(F^S))$.
*   **Space Projection:** The AT loss is computed between this projected student map and the teacher's map: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{AT}} = \text{MSE}(\hat{A}^S, A^T)$. The adapter learns to map the student's representation into the teacher's feature space, filtering structural mismatch noise.
*   **Zero Latency Overhead at Inference:** Upon training completion, the adapter $\Phi$ is discarded. The student model retains its original lightweight architecture with zero additional runtime cost.
